Sequential logic
D flip-flop: captures D input on the rising clock edge and holds it until the next edge.
T flip-flop: toggles its output on every clock edge when T is high — commonly used in counters.
JK flip-flop: J=1,K=0 sets output; J=0,K=1 resets; J=K=1 toggles.
Register: a group of binary cells (each cell stores one bit).
Binary cell: a device with two stable states, capable of storing one bit of information.
Parity bit: an extra bit attached to a message to make the total number of 1s either even or odd — useful for detecting transmission errors.
Combinational logic is "forgetful" — give it inputs, it gives you outputs, but the moment inputs change, so does the output. It has no memory of what happened before.
Sequential logic fixes this. It adds the ability to remember — making circuits that can store state, count, detect sequences, and respond to history, not just the present moment.
The key ingredient is the clock — a signal that ticks like a heartbeat, alternating between 0 and 1 at a fixed rate. Sequential circuits use this tick to decide when to act.
Latch vs flip-flop
A latch is the simpler of the two. It's "level-triggered" — it passes data through as long as its enable signal is high, like a door that stays open while you hold the handle. This makes it somewhat unpredictable in complex designs.
A flip-flop is more disciplined. It's "edge-triggered" — it only captures data at the exact moment the clock ticks (the rising edge). Think of it like a camera shutter: it takes one snapshot per tick and ignores everything in between. This predictability is why flip-flops are the building block of virtually every modern chip.
The four types

D flip-flop — the most common. Whatever is at input D gets copied to output Q on the clock edge. Clean and simple.
SR flip-flop — has a Set and Reset input. S=1 forces output high, R=1 forces it low. The forbidden state is both S and R = 1.
JK flip-flop — an improved SR that eliminates the forbidden state. When both inputs are 1, it toggles the output.
T flip-flop — a simplified JK. When T=1, output flips every clock tick. When T=0, it holds. Perfect for building counters.

What they're used for
Flip-flops are the atoms of memory. Chain them together and you get registers (store multi-bit values), counters (count clock ticks), and shift registers (move bits along one at a time). At a larger scale, they form the state elements of finite state machines — the logic that powers everything from traffic lights to CPUs.
